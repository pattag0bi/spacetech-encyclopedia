A first-principles breakdown · 12 chapters · 2026 edition

Everything in space comes down to one brutal equation.

Most write-ups of space technology just hand you a list of objects — rockets, satellites, stations, probes. That misses the whole point. Every one of those objects exists to answer one brutal physics problem: it takes a huge amount of energy to climb out of Earth's gravity well, and a rocket pays for that energy by burning most of its own body weight as fuel. This page follows that one idea all the way up — through raw materials, parts, machines, finished vehicles, orbit, and the signals they beam home — and ends up somewhere surprising: the real money in space isn't in the rocket at all.

00

The physics floor

Tier zero · non-negotiable

Four numbers explain almost everything that follows. Once you know them, the rest of this page is just people cleverly working around them.

§The energy is cheap. The delivery is not.

Here is a fact that should stop you in your tracks: lifting one kilogram to low orbit takes about 34 MJ — roughly 9.4 kWh, the same energy as leaving a 100-watt light bulb burning for about four days straight. At what your power company charges, that is worth under one dollar. And yet launch prices have historically run ten to fifty thousand times that. Almost none of the gap is the energy itself — in fact 90% of that energy is not even about climbing up, it is about going sideways fast enough — 7.8 km/s — to keep missing the ground as you fall around the planet. The real cost is that you must deliver it all in a few violent minutes, aboard a machine that carries its own oxygen supply and, until recently, was thrown away after one use.

Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation, 1903

How much speed a rocket can gain grows only as the logarithm of how much heavier it is fueled than empty. Flip that around, which is what actually matters when designing one, and it turns vicious: mass grows exponentially with the mission. Want 10% more speed? You do not add 10% more fuel — you multiply the fuel load.

Getting to orbit needs about 9.4 km/s of speed change in total — 7.8 for going fast enough sideways, plus roughly 1.6 wasted fighting gravity, air and steering on the way up. Feed that into a decent kerosene engine (Isp ≈ 340 s, a measure of fuel efficiency) and the rocket must be about 16.7:1, full versus empty. Only about six percent of what sits on the pad is allowed to be anything other than fuel — tanks, engines, wiring, nose cone, and your actual cargo, all squeezed into that sliver. The cargo alone is usually just 2% to 4%. This single number explains almost every strange thing about how spacecraft are built: the obsession with grams, the exotic metals, the solar panels that fold up like origami instead of just being bigger.

There are only two real ways out of the trap, and the whole industry is built around them. Staging drops the empty tanks as you climb, so the equation basically restarts with a lighter rocket each time. Reuse is sneakier — it does not make the equation any easier at all. What it does is let you fly the same expensive engine and tank a hundred times instead of once, dividing its cost by a hundred. You pay for that with performance, since fuel has to be held back to fly the booster home instead of burning it all going up, but you win on cost. That single trade-off has been the biggest commercial decision in rocketry over the last fifteen years.

01

Matter

Tier one · raw inputs

Only a handful of materials show up again and again in spacecraft, because physics simply does not give engineers a choice.

§Propellant — the biggest single thing you will ever buy

Here is something people do not expect: a rocket is basically a flying fuel tank with an engine bolted to the bottom. So choosing the fuel decides almost everything else — how big the vehicle is, how it is plumbed, what ground equipment it needs, whether it can be reused. The core trade-off is exhaust speed against density: hydrogen gives the fastest exhaust of any practical rocket fuel, but it is so light and fluffy that hydrogen stages end up huge, wrapped in thick insulation, and prone to leaking. Density matters most on the bottom stage, where drag and tank weight dominate. Exhaust speed (Isp) matters most on the top stage, where the rocket equation's exponent bites hardest.

§Structure — strong enough, light enough, cheap enough

Tanks & primary structure
Aluminium-lithium 2195

A lithium-aluminium alloy: lighter and stiffer than ordinary aircraft aluminium, and easy to weld. The standard choice for fuel tanks for thirty years.

Tanks & fairings
Carbon fibre composite

Gives the best strength for its weight of anything available, but the moulds are expensive and hidden damage is hard to detect. Used everywhere for nose cones and pressure tanks, still debated for holding super-cold fuel.

Reusable airframe
Stainless steel 304L

Heavier than the fancy alternatives for the same strength, but it stays strong when hot, is cheap, and can be welded outdoors in a tent. A trade nobody expected to work — extra weight for far less cost — and it did.

Hot section
Inconel 718 · GRCop

Nickel and copper alloys that let an engine's combustion chamber survive gas hotter than 3,300 °C, cooled by fuel running through channels just millimetres away.

Entry
PICA · carbon-carbon · tiles

Materials that either sacrifice themselves, burning away and taking the heat with them, or, like the tiles on a reusable capsule, are simply so bad at conducting heat that it never gets through. Reuse is pushing hard toward the tiles.

Thermal control
Kapton MLI · OSR

Shiny, layered blankets and mirrored panels. In a vacuum there is no air to carry heat away — the only way to lose it is to radiate it, like a glowing coal — so how shiny or dull a surface is is the cooling system.

Scarce and strategic

  • Plutonium-238 — the only practical fuel for a radioisotope generator, a heat source that turns its own decay into electricity for spacecraft too far from the Sun for solar panels to work. Production stopped for twenty years and only recently restarted, so it is supply, not engineering, that limits how many outer-planet missions can fly.
  • Helium — used to pressurise fuel tanks because nothing else stays gaseous at those freezing temperatures. A single launch can burn through a genuinely significant slice of the world's supply.
  • Xenon — a byproduct of separating industrial gases from air, and there simply is not enough of it to fuel thousands of satellites. Krypton, cheaper and slightly less efficient, is increasingly used instead.
  • Radiation-hardened chips — built on manufacturing processes a decade or more behind your phone's, in tiny quantities, and priced accordingly high.
02

Components

Tier two · parts

This is where weight, reliability, and cost actually get decided — one part at a time, knowing nobody can ever fix it once it is up there.

Nobody can walk out and fix a satellite, or even reach over and reboot it by hand. So every single part has to survive three hostile environments at once. First, launch itself — a few minutes of bone-rattling noise, shock and vibration. Second, the vacuum of space — no air means no convection, so materials slowly boil off their own molecules, metal parts pressed together can literally weld themselves shut, and in low orbit stray oxygen atoms chemically eat away at surfaces. Third, radiation — years of steady bombardment that degrades electronics, plus the occasional stray high-energy particle that can flip a single bit of memory or permanently short out a chip. The result is an industry that builds in tiny batches, tests brutally, and locks in the exact parts list years before anything flies.

The recurring component families

Power
Triple-junction GaAs cells

Cells made of gallium arsenide, layered three ways to catch more of the light spectrum. They turn 30–32% of sunlight into electricity, against 18–22% for the silicon on your roof, and shrug off radiation far better. Glued to panels with hinges that only ever have to work once — when they unfold.

Power
Li-ion packs · RTGs (radioisotope generators)

Batteries carry the spacecraft through darkness — in low orbit that means charging and draining about 16 times a day, tens of thousands of cycles over a lifetime. Past Jupiter, sunlight gets too weak to bother with, so a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, or RTG, takes over: a lump of plutonium-238 — chosen because it decays steadily over decades rather than in a sudden burst, giving off heat the whole time, and its radiation is weak alpha particles that a thin metal casing blocks completely. That steady heat is converted straight into electricity — no moving parts to wear out, no sunlight required. It is not power-plant plutonium and cannot be used in a weapon; its only job on a spacecraft is to keep making warmth long after the Sun is too faint to help.

Attitude
Reaction wheels · CMGs (control moment gyroscopes)
A reaction wheel pushes the spacecraft by changing its own spin rate. A CMG spins at constant speed and tilts the whole wheel on a gimbal instead — much stronger for the same size.

Spin up a heavy flywheel inside the spacecraft and, just like a skater pulling in their arms, the whole spacecraft gently rotates the other way. A reaction wheel just changes its spin speed to do this; a control moment gyroscope (CMG) keeps spinning at constant speed and instead tilts the whole spinning wheel on a gimbal, which pushes back much harder for the same size and power — the choice for large spacecraft like the ISS that need to slew serious mass. Either way, the spin builds up over time and has to be dumped using magnets or thrusters. If the wheel itself breaks, there is often no backup.

Attitude
Star trackers · IMUs (inertial measurement units) · sun sensors

A star tracker is basically a camera that photographs the night sky and matches the pattern of stars against a catalogue, pinning down exactly which way the spacecraft is pointing to within a sliver of a degree. An IMU is the fast, dumb backup: gyroscopes and accelerometers that feel the spacecraft turning and drift, updating many times a second with no need to see anything — accurate for only seconds before it drifts, but never blind. Everything else on board is a rougher, faster backup for when the star tracker's view is not available.

Compute
Rad-hard processors · FPGAs

Computers that triple-check their own logic, correct memory errors on the fly, and have a watchdog that reboots them if they freeze. Increasingly paired with cheap ordinary chips for the heavy number-crunching, flown on the assumption those chips will occasionally crash — and just get rebooted.

Fluidics
Valves · COPVs (composite overwrapped pressure vessels) · turbopumps

Tanks wrapped in carbon fibre hold gas at over 300 times atmospheric pressure while staying as light as possible. A turbopump, spinning at 30,000 rpm, can shove an entire swimming pool's worth of fuel through in a single minute.

03

Machines

Tier three · subsystems that do work

Really only two machines matter here: the thing that makes thrust, and the platform that keeps everything else alive.

§Engine cycles — where does the pump get its power?

To burn fuel efficiently it has to be squeezed into the chamber at very high pressure, which means pumping it there first. So where does the energy to run that pump come from? That single question defines an engine's "cycle," and it decides how efficient, how complicated, and how expensive the whole engine ends up being.

§The engine atlas — every kind of rocket engine, taken apart

Now take them apart one at a time — not just the liquid cycles, but solid motors and hybrids, the small thrusters that steer spacecraft, the nozzles that shape the exhaust, electric and nuclear propulsion, and the engines that breathe air, ride sunlight or dream of fusion. Pick a family and an engine, press Next, and follow the propellant from tank to exhaust. Then run it yourself in the physics lab and watch what the real equations do. Where real hardware exists, load it and see how close first principles get.

§The bus — a life-support system for an instrument

Split any satellite in two and you get the payload — the actual reason it exists, whether that is a camera, a radio transponder, or a telescope — and the bus, everything else: the life-support system that keeps the payload powered, pointed the right way, cool enough, in touch with the ground, and in the right orbit. Strip away the payload, and the bus is roughly the same six systems whether the satellite weighs four kilograms or six tonnes. That is exactly why manufacturers can mass-produce them like any other product.

§Spacecraft by class

0.1–10 kg
CubeSat · 1U–12U

A standardised 10-centimetre cube. This one idea made it possible to hitch a ride on someone else's rocket and build satellites out of ordinary off-the-shelf electronics. It turned university labs into real satellite operators.

10–500 kg
Smallsat · constellation node

Built on an assembly line and launched by the hundreds, treated as disposable rather than precious. Backup lives in the swarm, not in any single satellite — a complete reversal of how spacecraft used to be built.

2,000–6,500 kg
GEO communications satellite

Built to last 15 years, climbing to its final orbit slowly on electric thrusters, carrying hundreds of radio channels. This was the original space business, and it is now fighting off competition from low-orbit internet constellations.

Varies
Deep-space probe

Has to think for itself, because it is so far away that light itself takes hours to arrive — real-time joystick control from Earth is simply impossible. Runs on a plutonium heat source or enormous solar panels, and can spend a decade just cruising to its destination.

Crewed
Capsule · lander · rover

Adds air, water, an emergency escape system, and a reliability bar set roughly a hundred times higher than cargo — which is why it costs roughly a hundred times more per kilogram.

400+ t
Space station

A permanently crewed laboratory, built piece by piece in orbit and kept stocked by a steady stream of resupply ships. The ISS is the biggest thing humans have ever assembled outside Earth — a standing lab for studying life without gravity.

05

Access & orbits

Tier five · the transaction

Launching a rocket is not the goal — choosing an orbit is. And that single choice decides what business the satellite can even be in.

How high you fly locks in four things at once, and there is no picking just one: latency (how long a signal takes to get there and back), coverage (how much of Earth one satellite can see), revisit (how often it swings back over the same spot), and lifetime (how fast leftover atmosphere will drag it down and burn it up). Park a satellite at the special altitude where it orbits once every 24 hours, so it just hangs motionless over one spot on Earth, and it covers a third of the planet — but a round trip signal takes a quarter of a second, because the satellite is 35,786 km away. Fly low instead and the signal barely takes any time to arrive, but that same satellite drops over the horizon after just ten minutes, so hundreds or thousands of them are needed to keep continuous coverage.

Moving between orbits

  • Hohmann transfer — two short burns trace a stretched oval path connecting one circular orbit to another. The cheapest possible way from A to B in space, and the reason launch windows exist: you have to wait for the planets to line up right.
  • Plane change — tilting your orbital plane is shockingly expensive (swinging it just 30° in low orbit costs about as much fuel as heading to the Moon), which is why where and which direction you launch from are strategic decisions, not mere logistics.
  • Gravity assist — swing close past a planet and steal a sliver of its own momentum as it orbits the Sun. Free extra speed, paid for not in fuel but in years of extra flight time and rigid, unmovable launch dates.
  • Aerobraking / aerocapture — let a planet's own atmosphere do your braking instead of burning fuel. Arriving at Mars this way saves several km/s of speed for free; the price is a heat shield and a few nail-biting minutes where nothing can be done but wait.
  • Electric orbit raising — a gentle push, about one ten-thousandth of Earth's gravity, sustained for months. It can cut a satellite's launch weight in half, at the cost of delaying its first paycheck by roughly a third of a year.
06

Operations

Tier six · midstream

Half of this whole industry never leaves the ground — and it is the half that never gets a day off.

The ground segment — antennas, radios, mission control rooms, scheduling software, orbit tracking — is a bigger business than launch itself, and about as big as building the satellites. Think of a constellation as a distributed computer network, except its nodes happen to be flying at 7.8 km/s. Which means the hard problems are exactly the ones you would expect from that description: scheduling who talks to whom and when, routing data around, pushing out software updates, and always knowing exactly where every node is.

Function
Tracking & orbit determination

Measuring distance, the Doppler shift of a radio signal, and radar returns pin down exactly where a satellite is right now; software then predicts where it will be next. Every collision warning and every antenna's pointing angle depends on getting this right.

Function
TT&C and mission control

Health data streams down, commands stream up. This is where operators catch problems, coax a satellite out of an emergency safe mode, and increasingly push software updates that add whole new capabilities after launch.

Function
Deep Space Network

Three giant antenna complexes, dishes 34 to 70 metres across, spaced a third of the way around the globe from each other, so as Earth spins there is always one facing any given spacecraft. A single shared resource that everyone always wants more time on than there is.

Function
Station keeping

Satellites parked in that 24-hour orbit constantly nudge themselves to stay inside a tiny box in the sky, fighting the gentle push of sunlight and the tug of the Moon and Sun. Low-orbit satellites instead fight atmospheric drag. Either way, whatever fuel is left on board is how much life the satellite has left.

Function
End of life

By law, a dead satellite must come down and burn up within 25 years, soon to be tightened to 5, or, if it is in that far-out 24-hour orbit, get boosted 300 km further to a graveyard orbit where it will not bother anyone. A satellite's very last manoeuvre is no longer optional — it is a legal requirement.

Emerging
Servicing & refuelling

A new kind of mission: sending a robot to dock with a satellite that was never designed to be docked with, to refuel it, repair it, or nudge it somewhere new. Turns a satellite from a disposable object into something that can actually be maintained.

Signal & data

Tier seven · the actual product

Almost every satellite, no matter what it is for, is really just selling data. The physics of getting that data safely down to the ground is the second great equation on this page.

A radio signal loses power with the square of the distance it travels, and the square of its frequency. From that 24-hour orbit, broadcasting at 12 GHz, the loss adds up to about 205 dB — the signal that lands on your dish is roughly one hundred billion billion times weaker than what was sent.

Basically every trick in satellite communications is an attempt to claw back some of that lost signal: bigger dishes that focus more of it, more powerful transmitters, quieter receiver electronics, clever error-correcting codes that squeeze the last usable bit out of a noisy signal, and — most powerfully — flying closer. Drop from that 24-hour orbit down to 550 km and about 36 dB of loss disappears — the signal arrives four thousand times stronger. That one fact, plus cheap mass-produced satellites and cheap rockets, is the entire reason low-orbit internet constellations exist.

The other big lever is reusing the same radio frequencies over and over. A single wide beam covering a continent has to share its limited bandwidth among everyone underneath it. Split that into hundreds of narrow beams, each covering its own small patch of ground, and the same frequencies can be reused in every patch that is not right next to another one using them, multiplying total capacity without needing any new spectrum. It is the same trick that turned old car phones into modern cell networks.

08

Applications

Tier eight · downstream

There are four things you can really only do from orbit: broadcast to everyone at once, keep perfect universal time, watch the whole planet, and do physics nowhere else allows.

§Connect — one transmitter, a continent of receivers

Being high up buys a clear line of sight to millions of receivers at once. That is why satellites are unbeatable for broadcast (identical content to everyone simultaneously), for covering places where it is simply not worth building cell towers (oceans, deserts, polar regions, aeroplanes), and now, via low-orbit constellations, for genuinely fast internet. The newest trick is direct-to-device: a satellite convincingly pretending to be a nearby cell tower, so an ordinary unmodified phone can connect straight to it.

§Locate — position as a side effect of telling time

A navigation satellite really only does one thing: it broadcasts the exact time, with extreme precision, along with exactly where it is. Your phone listens to four of these broadcasts at once and solves four equations for four unknowns — position in three dimensions, plus how far off its own cheap clock is. That trick is what lets a one-dollar chip give you the accuracy of an atomic clock it does not even contain. Several systems now do this — America's GPS, Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo, China's BeiDou, and regional systems in India and Japan. But here is the twist: the side effect turned out to matter more than the main feature. Free, precise, universal time from these satellites now underpins how banks settle transactions, how power grids stay synchronised, and how phone networks run — and losing that signal is treated as a genuine national emergency.

§Observe — the only vantage point that sees everything, again and again

Sensor
Optical / multispectral

Ordinary photographs from space, as sharp as under a metre per pixel, or coarser colour images split into a few bands. Cheap and easy to understand, but useless through clouds or at night.

Sensor
Synthetic aperture radar

Synthetic aperture radar: the satellite brings its own light source, so it sees straight through clouds and darkness, faking a giant antenna by combining signals as it moves along its path. Combine two passes cleverly and ground shifting by mere millimetres shows up.

Sensor
Hyperspectral

Instead of one colour per pixel, hundreds of narrow slices of the light spectrum — a full rainbow fingerprint for every point on the ground. That fingerprint identifies actual materials: a crop under stress, a mineral deposit, even a leaking plume of methane.

Sensor
Thermal infrared

Reads the temperature of the ground itself — spotting wildfires, mapping how much hotter cities run than the countryside around them, tracking irrigation, and imaging at night when visible light cannot.

Sensor
Radar altimetry / gravimetry

Bounces radar straight down to measure sea level to within centimetres, or measures Earth's gravity field itself, subtle enough to detect an ice sheet losing mass or an aquifer slowly draining away underground.

Sensor
GNSS radio occultation

Watches how a navigation satellite's signal bends as it grazes through the atmosphere on its way to another satellite, and uses that bending to map temperature and humidity layer by layer. Cheap, precise, and now a major ingredient in weather forecasts.

§Discover — when the environment itself is the instrument

Above the atmosphere there is no air to blur the view, no ozone or water vapour swallowing whole slices of light, no clouds, and no day-night cycle to work around. Space telescopes take advantage of that to see wavelengths — infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma-ray — that are physically blocked from ever reaching the ground. Probes fly right up to other planets because photographing them from a distance can only tell you so much. And weightlessness itself is a laboratory condition unavailable anywhere on Earth: growing purer protein crystals, pulling fibre-optic cable without the distortions gravity-driven currents cause, growing tissue cultures that do not just sink to the bottom of their dish — plus the long-term data on how human bodies hold up, which any future trip beyond the Moon depends on.

Spinoff — technology that leaked back down

Solving problems this hard tends to spit out useful technology as a side effect. The camera sensor in your phone traces straight back to a spacecraft imaging chip. So does memory foam, cordless power tools, portable water filters, scratch-resistant glasses, freeze-dried food, certain LED medical treatments, insulin pumps, and fireproof fabric. But honestly, the biggest spinoff is not a gadget at all — it is a way of thinking: the whole discipline of engineering a system so carefully, and testing it so thoroughly, that it simply cannot be allowed to fail.

09

Value capture

Tier nine · where the money is

Follow the actual money, and this whole chain flips upside down.

Out of a global space economy worth roughly $600 billion, about three quarters is ordinary commercial business, not government spending, and the overwhelming majority of that commercial money sits downstream: satellite services and the equipment used to receive them. Launching rockets — the part everyone photographs, the part that gets the headlines — is only a few percent of the total. The right way to think about launch is as an entry fee that gates everything built on top of it. A tenfold drop in launch cost does not matter because launch itself is a huge business — it matters because it changes what becomes possible to build in every market stacked on top of it.

The three business models that survive

  • Cost-plus programmes. A government pays for something no private company would build on its own — visiting other planets, national security, sending humans beyond Earth. Few units, extremely high standards, funding that survives for decades because it stays politically popular.
  • Infrastructure with subscribers. Spend a fortune building a satellite network, then sell subscriptions to use it. Needs huge upfront capital, gets more valuable as more users join, and historically has a habit of bankrupting whoever builds it first, right before someone else buys the leftover satellites cheap and makes it profitable.
  • Data products. Do not sell raw images or bandwidth — sell the answer: crop yield forecasts, ship tracking, methane leak alerts, insurance risk pricing. The highest profit margins, the least money needed up front, and the hardest part is just finding customers who will pay for it.
10

Constraints

Tier ten · the limits

These days it is not the engineering holding this industry back — it is a handful of real-world scarcities and one plain physical danger.

Physical · escalating
Orbital debris

About 40,000 objects bigger than a fist are currently tracked in orbit, plus well over a million smaller pieces nobody is watching. At orbital speed, 10 km/s, even a fragment the size of a grape carries the punch of a hand grenade. The nightmare scenario, called Kessler syndrome, is a chain reaction: one collision makes debris, that debris causes more collisions, and a whole slice of orbit could become unusable for generations.

Legal · finite
Spectrum & slots

Radio frequencies and parking spots in that 24-hour orbit are handed out internationally on a first-come, first-served, use-it-or-lose-it basis. Simply filing a claim for one is a real, valuable, tradeable asset, and the queue to file has itself become a competitive battlefield.

Environmental
Radiation & vacuum

Years of steady radiation slowly wear down solar panels and electronics; the odd high-energy particle can flip a bit of memory; a single bad solar storm can end a mission in a day. Because there is no air in space, there is no convection either, so keeping a spacecraft at the right temperature comes down entirely to balancing heat radiated away against heat absorbed, while stray oxygen atoms in low orbit slowly eat away at exposed plastics.

Regulatory
Export control & licensing

Almost any piece of space hardware could also be a missile part — that is just true by nature. International export rules end up shaping supply chains, partnerships, and even which specific components an engineer in a given country is legally allowed to touch. Often that legal constraint is a bigger obstacle than the engineering itself.

Safety
Energy density

A fully fuelled rocket is one of the biggest concentrations of raw energy humans ever assemble outside a nuclear weapon. That is exactly why launches have exclusion zones, safety officers, and self-destruct systems built in — when something goes wrong, it tends to go wrong as an explosion, as a Delta II rocket demonstrated when it blew up on the pad in 1997.

Astronomical
Dark and quiet skies

Thousands of satellites streaking across the sky leave bright trails that ruin long-exposure astronomical photos, and their transmissions can bleed into frequencies reserved for radio telescopes. Builders now routinely design around this — dulling reflective coatings, tilting satellites at dawn and dusk when they are most visible, and avoiding certain radio bands altogether.
